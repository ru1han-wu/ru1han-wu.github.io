The problem

Why does it matter where the hand lands?

A robot can lift a mug and still leave someone reaching for a blocked handle. Sometimes the request names the part to grasp. Sometimes the robot has to infer which part should stay free: the rim you drink from, the body of a hot pan, the handle meant for your hand.

reads the request, picks a human-like grasp that respects it, and uses touch to correct its fingers as they close. It is trained in simulation and runs on a Unitree G1-D humanoid with an Inspire RH56DFTP tactile hand.

From the paper

Abstract

Recent advances in vision-language-action models and world models have driven rapid progress in dexterous grasping. However, compliant success, which we refer to as lifting and holding an object while satisfying contact requirements arising from social etiquette, interpersonal interaction norms, and explicit instructions, remains an open challenge. For example, handing over a knife requires keeping the hand off the sharp edge and leaving the handle free for the recipient. To address this problem, we present , a plan-then-refine framework that converts contact intent into a human-like grasp plan and adapts it online through tactile feedback. Specifically, (i) first encodes explicit and implicit requirements in a unified part-level contact specification. (ii) An efficient vision-language decision model then jointly predicts this specification and a human gesture prior, which is regularized by a conservative robot capability bound to favor natural and reliable gestures. (iii) A compliance model then evaluates gesture-part pairs to select a grasp that balances human preference, robot reliability, and contact compliance. (iv) Furthermore, we leverage tactile feedback to refine finger closure and guide failure recovery, thereby yielding a closed-loop solution with improved robustness. To facilitate thorough evaluation of compliant success across diverse object categories and scenarios, we introduce DexComply, a benchmark covering both explicit and implicit contact requirements. Extensive experiments on DexComply and a public dataset show that outperforms state-of-the-art methods by significant margins. trained only in simulation also enables zero-shot transfer to a real humanoid.
